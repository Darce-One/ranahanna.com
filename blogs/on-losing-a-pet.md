---
title: On losing a pet
date: 2024-08-21
thumbnail: on-losing-a-pet.webp
---

A few weeks ago I lost my dog Lili, although to call her my dog would be inexact. I was simply one of Lili’s multiple guardians. My only claim to fame is that I did not give up on her.

I adopted Lili from the shelter in February 2020. She was nine years old. The collapse of Lebanese politics, economics, finance and society was well underway by then and people, unable to sustain the expenses of raising a pet, were abandoning them daily. I adopted her out of a need to help alleviate our predicament in any way I could, as a way to exert some influence on a situation that had long spiraled out of anyone’s control. And that was before Covid even.

I wanted to do my bit for society and the only way I knew how was to take a dog off the hands of the animal shelters, battered as they were by the now-established crisis. As I did not want to potty-train a puppy again, I thought I would adopt a senior dog and give them a chance at love again. I had read many stories about people rescuing dogs and being, in turn, themselves rescued by these dogs. Perhaps, I must have thought, there would be a reciprocal benefit there.

Lili did not rescue me in any way. After spending the first three months hiding under chairs and humping my husband’s leg, she launched her campaign to make my life ten times more difficult than it already was. A peeing, pooping, barking and biting machine (toes and noses were her specialty), Lili alienated me from most of what was near and dear to me, including members of my family, my friends and my children’s friends. She also made my house smell like a subway station late at night.

But she broke my heart. With a face that was too small for her body, she was anxious, constantly pacing around the house and going as far as gnawing her fur off her body. Judging by how far she jumped every time I tried to caress her, I assumed that she had probably been mistreated in her youth. I even pitied her failed attempt to assert her dominance over our resident queen Labrador who would have none of it and simply flicked her away with her tail. Lili broke my heart because she had so often been abandoned and neglected, although six months into her tenancy with us I could see why. I was tempted to abandon her myself. But it was too late, I had given her my word.

To say that I adopted Lili at the most challenging period of my life is to understate the changes we, as Lebanese, were going through. In August 2020 a huge part of Beirut shattered and with it our homes and our hearts. We had to act, move, and rebuild quickly when all we wanted to do was give up. The last thing I could handle was an unsure, scared, anxiety-ridden dog. I was physically unable to pick up our pieces and hers. But I had given her my word.

We decided to leave Lebanon for Cyprus within a few weeks and Lili, without travel papers yet, had to wait. A year later I was able to bring her with me to Cyprus where she set out to terrorize the few family and friends I had there. She also lay at my feet as I wrote and followed me everywhere, from my desk to the bathroom, where she waited anxiously outside and lay again at my feet once back at my desk, making sure she doesn’t get abandoned again.

Lili lived with us (or against us) for four and a half years. Only in the last year did I see her smile (on a trip to the dog beach), grow an appetite (especially for watermelon) and lie on her back (once) — but what she did often, was stand for petting. She had stopped barking at the door and left people’s toes and noses well alone. She was almost deaf and blind with advanced Cushing’s disease. She had become so frail that we had to carry her on her walks. I made a mental note to buy her a cart once back from holiday.

There are two essential problems with mourning a pet: for one, it is relative. In the grand scheme of things, with wars raging and children dying and people suffering on a daily basis, the loss of a pet, adopted, rescued or bought seems — how can I say this delicately? — frivolous. Perhaps due to this relativity, mourning a pet is often a very lonely endeavour. Few people, even those who are themselves pet lovers, can understand the emptiness and loneliness that accompanies the loss of a creature that does not eat, walk or communicate as we do.

Perhaps I do not cry for Lili. Perhaps, like Letizia Nazarena in Gabriel Garzia Marquez’ *The Autumn of the Patriarch*, I cry for tumultuous times past, for my country as it descends deeper into chaos, for the trouble in the world, for the children. For myself.

As I sit and write this, Lili no longer sleeps at my feet. I did not get the opportunity to buy the cart, nor to say goodbye. Lili died peacefully while being held. I hope that she knew that, despite all her faults, she was loved.

I will miss her terribly.
