When my son received rejections from his two top-choice universities in the UK, he was relieved. Because to be perfectly honest, these were not his top two choices — they were mine. His real top choice was to go to Milan, where he had already secured an offer. And so, having overcome my initial disappointment, I found myself feeling something unexpected: happiness.

Here’s why.

## Hard Work, Talent, and Life Lessons

My son was rejected because of his grades: one A\*, a predicted A\*, and two Cs. The A\*s are in Maths and Further Maths — subjects he excelled in, thanks to an incredible teacher who ignited his passion and helped him realize his natural affinity for the subject. The Cs? Well, let’s just say his effort in those subjects was… selective. For reasons that are, and remain, his own, he simply didn’t apply himself in those areas.

And that brings me to my second reason for being glad. I’m hoping this experience will reinforce a fundamental truth: that talent alone isn’t enough. That hard work, when paired with natural ability, can lead to incredible results. And I hope he’s now mature enough to embrace that lesson.

## Did These Universities Make the Right Call?

It depends.

Universities, particularly those in the UK, use school grades as the first barrier to entry. It’s a safe bet — they want students who will deliver consistent results, especially those paying local UK tuition fees. It makes sense. They’re protecting their reputation, their rankings, and their bottom line.

But here’s where context matters.

In 2020, Beirut exploded. And with it, our home.

For my husband and me, that was the final push to move our then 14-year-old son to Cyprus, away from the destruction and uncertainty in Lebanon at the time. It wasn’t just a change of location — it was a total upheaval. New country. New home. New school. A completely different education system. And on top of it all, Covid lockdowns.

He didn’t speak the language. He didn’t know anyone. As he wrote in his personal statement, it was an opportunity he did not want.

So, like many teenagers in lockdown, he coped the only way he knew how: by playing Call of Duty with his friends back home, having already spent the year prior playing Fifa. He didn’t yet have the maturity or foresight to understand that the effort (or lack of it) he was putting in at 14 would come back to shape his future at 18. And when I, in my role as his “in-house counselor,” tried to push him on the matter, he fired me. Twice.

## What These Universities Are Missing

Fast forward four years, and my son is a mature, well-adjusted adult. He is resilient, multilingual, and highly adaptable. He’s built friendships, carved out his place in the world, and developed a killer problem-solving mindset that will serve him for life. He is also extremely fun to be around.

So did those UK universities make the right call?

It depends.

If their goal is to play it safe, then yes. They passed on a candidate who didn’t fit their selection criteria and that’s fair enough.

But if their goal is to cultivate brilliant, globally minded, adaptable individuals — then I think they may have missed out on a great recruit.

Their loss. Milan’s gain.
