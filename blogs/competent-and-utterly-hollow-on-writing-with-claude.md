This is what Claude said when I challenged it this morning. I went to Claude feeling utter despair at the current state of the world. I was overwhelmed.

“I can’t understand a thing,” I said. I can’t make sense of the world around me!

Claude reassured me that I was not alone in my distress and that contrary to me feeling stupid, I was actually simply self-aware (big win for Claude). And then, like a very good therapist Claude guided me slowly, patiently to the one question that was on my mind this morning: is technology making us dumber or more intelligent?

I did not wake up desperate but was triggered by an article in the Financial Times newspaper about the use of AI technology in warfare. As I saw it, the world was coming undone while I was eating my breakfast eggs. The irony of going to Claude after this trigger is not lost on me (cue Anthropic vs. The Pentagon).

My fear of, and disdain for, technology, had been building up gradually specifically through my work as a university admissions consultant. I always start my process with a questionnaire and invariably I always ask: where do you get your news from? And the answer invariably is: Instagram.

The world is changing fast. Too fast for us normal mortals to keep up with it and understand it completely. And yet, at a time when we need to push our brainpower into warp speed, many of my students are instead relegating it to algorithms and predictive models. And I don’t know what to tell them because I myself am ambivalent about the technology. See above.

My ambivalence toward various social media and LLMs stems from the following: on the one hand we have access to a lot of free (both literally and figuratively) information. On the other hand, we have access to a lot of free (both literally and figuratively) information. One coin, same two sides. Students I am working with today are not only overloaded with input, they are overloaded with input whose value is dubious. Many of them often do not yet have the skills to discern between what is of value and what isn’t. The polarizing effects of social media, whether Instagram, X or other, is not helping.

The issue I have with LLMs is that on the one hand they are extremely useful thought partners, and not necessarily because of their analytical ability, if for nothing then to force us to verbalize our thoughts. On the other hand, any LLM is also too willing to help out when asked. An LLM is all too happy to write your personal statement and college essay for you. Claude did not deny that it could write this article for me but it did point out that the output, while technically correct, may not channel my voice, hence my anxiety, properly. Competent, yet utterly hollow.

I have recently decided to phase out my consulting work and have done so for several reasons. One of these is that I honestly don’t know what to tell fearful, anxious students and their fearful, anxious parents looking to me for assurances that their child’s chosen degree is the one that proffers the biggest ROI. Another reason is that I am unable to convince my students that success takes effort. And time. And patience. And most of the time: silence.

So, as I slowly phase out my practice, what I want to leave my overstimulated, anxious students and their overstimulated, anxious parents with, is this:

There is no clear return on investment when it comes to university education. Your education is what you make of it. You could be a student at a top university but if you’re uncommitted, your outcomes are not necessarily better than an involved student at a lower-ranked university.

To fret is human. To be anxious about the future is normal, specifically in these times. Control what you can. The system is getting harder and harder to game. Know your strengths and your values and build on them. Mediocrity is not an option.

In times of uncertainty, our strength lies not in having the answers, but in asking the right questions. Do not take for granted what you are told (especially on social media!). Be discerning, challenge yourself and those around you. Start by challenging what you’re reading here. Progress comes from clash and synthesis, not from indoctrination.

If you’re spending four hours a day on social media, the only place you can hope to be successful is social media. The real world is happening outside your screen, not on it.

Get informed from as many sources as you can. It is the only way to build critical thinking. Go deeper than the headline on a subject that interests you.

The world is nuanced and cannot be explained in 30-second reels.

Do not outsource your thinking. Scrolling is great for inspiration. LLMs are great thinking partners. But throwing an idea you got from one technology to be fleshed out by another technology is to take the human out of the equation.

And that’s the biggest risk of all.

Rana Hanna is a Lebanese novelist and former university admissions consultant. Her debut novel *Birds in the Rain*, set against the 2006 Lebanese-Israeli war, was published by Bold Story Press in 2025. She holds an M.Phil. in Middle Eastern Studies from St. Antony’s College, Oxford, and a BA in Politics and History from the University of Nottingham.
