Every year, I meet students desperate to get into a certain university, or league of universities, because they believe that said university, let’s call it University X, will lead to Job Y, which will result in Lifestyle Z. Many of them skip a crucial step: How to get into University X in the first place.

There is a certain misconception among many high school students and their parents — that university admission is a reward for being good at jumping through hoops. That if you have certain grades and filled in the blanks for the requisite activities, you’re pretty much there. The reality is different. Top universities don’t just want students who follow the rules. They want students who already embody the people they believe belong there.

Take Neil deGrasse Tyson as an example. Before he was America’s most famous astrophysicist, he was a teenager with a telescope and an obsession with the stars. He gave lectures at local planetariums and wrote to scientists at NASA. His grades were, by his own admission, “not spectacular” — in fact, he says he was a B-student. But every Ivy League school chased him. Why? Because he wasn’t waiting for a prestigious name to make him interesting — he already was interested, and hence, interesting.

In an application pool filled with able and capable students, the ones who stand out are the ones already living the life they want to grow into.

If you’re aiming to study medicine, you don’t need to wait until university to shadow a doctor or volunteer in a hospital. If you’re into business, you don’t need a degree to launch a small venture or help a local charity with marketing. You like architecture? Start drawing. You want to be a writer? Write.

In other words: you want to go to the cool place? Do the cool things first. The shoes won’t make you run faster so to speak.

To be cool is to be confidently and authentically yourself in a way that draws others in — not by trying to impress, but by living with clarity, curiosity, and purpose.

It’s not about what you wear or who you know (although that helps), but how you carry yourself, how you think, and how you treat others. Cool people tend to be quietly passionate, independent thinkers, and unafraid to do things differently — even if no one’s watching.

Being cool in this sense is exactly what makes you stand out to universities. It signals motivation, ownership of your learning, and a willingness to dive into your interests — without needing a guaranteed reward.

One way to begin exploring your path is by doing a few psychometric tests. Tools like Strengths surveys, the Holland Code, or the Sparketype assessment can give you a clearer sense of your natural inclinations and strengths. You might discover that you enjoy solving problems, leading others, creating new things, or diving deep into data. That’s great information — but it’s only the beginning. Nothing beats doing. Use those insights as a starting point, then try something real. Build, write, volunteer, research, ask questions. The clarity will come from the experience, not just the test results.

One of the most powerful ways to grow and signal curiosity is by simply talking to people and reading. Reach out to professionals in fields that interest you. Ask questions. Listen. Read books and articles that pull you in and make you think. You may not be able to do brain surgery or run a courtroom at sixteen, but you can learn a great deal about what drives people in those careers.

Due of their age and experience, young students rarely get the chance to fully experience a job — and that’s okay. The goal isn’t to “tick a box” or prematurely commit to one path. The goal is to follow your interests and build your awareness. That’s what real preparation looks like.

Here are a few ideas to get you started:

- Start a blog or podcast about something you genuinely care about.
- Design an app or build a website that solves a small problem in your community.
- Read wildly and widely — not just schoolbooks, but biographies, science fiction, graphic novels, economic theory, you name it.
- Take a free online course in a subject you’ve never studied before.
- Volunteer in settings that challenge your worldview.
- Reach out to someone with a job you admire and ask for 15 minutes of their time to hear their story.

None of these require permission, a perfect GPA, or a clear endgame. What they require is curiosity and initiative.

And here’s the bonus: these activities don’t just help you get into university. They help you figure out who you are and what matters to you. In a world where the job you’ll end up doing might not even exist yet, that’s a serious advantage.

When we focus only on the end goal — “If I do this, I’ll get that” — we miss the value of doing something simply because it’s worth doing. Growth happens in the doing, not in the reward.

So if you’re a student (or a parent of one), don’t obsess over crafting the perfect university application. Instead, focus on becoming the kind of person a university wants to accept. The one they cannot say no to. That doesn’t come from ticking boxes. It comes from doing the cool things first.

Because the students who succeed are rarely the ones who waited to be told what to do. They’re the ones who went out and did it anyway.
