There is an epic scene at the end of the movie Finding Nemo where, after all their daring escapes and narrow misses with predators, Nemo and his friends finally break out of the dentist’s office aquarium. Their plan was so focused on escaping that when they actually succeed — finding themselves bobbing in Sydney Harbor inside plastic bags — they share a moment of bewildered silence before Bloat the pufferfish asks the inevitable question: “Now what?”

Similarly, after an all-too-often anxiety-ridden application process, students get their coveted place at their top university of choice. There’s celebration, relief, and then the question that begs itself: “Now what?”

## The Beginning, Not the End

For too many students and parents, university or college acceptance feels like crossing a finish line. It’s treated as the culmination of years of effort, the prize at the end of a long journey. But this mindset misses something crucial: getting into college isn’t the destination — it’s barely even the starting point.

The working landscape is changing rapidly, and in today’s rapidly evolving world, a college degree — even from a prestigious institution — is no longer a guaranteed ticket to success. The modern job market is undergoing significant transformations, with traditional industries becoming more competitive and new career paths emerging.

According to Forbes, 52% of college graduates are working in jobs that don’t necessitate higher education, and 75% remain in such positions a decade after graduating. In the UK, the graduate wage premium has diminished, especially for those in humanities fields. Many graduates face challenges securing employment, highlighting a disconnect between higher education and labor market demands. Newspapers widely publish articles about graduates of prestigious universities not finding employment after graduation. In my own practice, I am increasingly approached by students seeking to do a Master’s degree for lack of knowing doing what to do next.

## A Changing Landscape

Consider these realities:

- The World Economic Forum estimates that 65% of children entering primary school today will ultimately work in job types that don’t yet exist.
- The average person changes careers (not just jobs) 5–7 times during their working life.
- Employers consistently report that recent graduates lack critical thinking, problem-solving, and communication skills — despite their impressive grades and GPAs.

This isn’t meant to discourage you or your student. Rather, it’s a call to shift perspective. College isn’t merely the next box to check off, but rather a unique laboratory for growth, exploration, and intentional skill development.

## From Passive to Proactive

In the book *Designing Your Life*, authors and Stanford professors Bill Burnett and Dave Evans argue compellingly that we need to approach our careers with the same intentionality that designers bring to creating products. They emphasize “wayfinding” over following prescribed paths, and “building your way forward” rather than waiting for clarity to magically arrive.

Similarly, in their book *Hacking College: Why the Major Doeasn’t Matter — and What Really Does*, authors Ned Scott Laff and Scott Carlson offer strategies for students to maximize their college experience by taking ownership of their learning journey. Both resources emphasize a fundamental shift: from being passive consumers (of education) to becoming active architects of experience.

## What Does This Look Like in Practice?

For students entering college, taking ownership might include:

- Seeking out mentorship relationships with professors in areas of interest
- Pursuing research opportunities as early as their first year
- Crafting unique interdisciplinary studies rather than simply checking off degree requirements
- Using summers strategically for internships, skill development, or exploration
- Building meaningful projects outside of class requirements
- Creating a personal learning curriculum beyond assigned coursework

For parents, supporting this shift means:

- Asking different questions (“What are you curious about?” rather than “How are your grades?”)
- Encouraging thoughtful risk-taking and exploration
- Helping students develop systems for managing their newfound independence
- Being a sounding board rather than a problem-solver
- Understanding that some of the most valuable college experiences happen outside the classroom

## Beyond the Plastic Bag

Back to Nemo and his friends, bobbing in Sydney Harbor. Their story didn’t end with escape — they had to figure out how to break out of their plastic bags and navigate their way home. The real adventure was just beginning.

Similarly, college acceptance is just one step on a much longer journey. The real question isn’t “Did you get in?” but rather “What will you do with this opportunity?” Or as I sometimes like to ask more bluntly: “How will you get out?”

The students who thrive in today’s world aren’t necessarily those who attended the most prestigious universities or graduated with perfect GPAs. They’re the ones who approached their education with intentionality, who sought out challenges rather than avoiding them, who built meaningful skills through experience, and who learned to navigate uncertainty with confidence.

So as you and your student celebrate those well-deserved acceptance letters, take a moment to appreciate the achievement — and then start asking the more important question: “Now what?” The answer will be different for every student, but the approach should be the same: intentional, proactive, and built on taking ownership of the journey ahead.
